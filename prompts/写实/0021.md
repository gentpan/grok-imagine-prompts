## English

**Somewhere in a factory on**

Somewhere in a factory on a parcel of land that was once a country a machine broke down. It wasn’t a very important machine as these things go. It folded metal into a shape that fed into a funnel, that dropped onto a conveyor belt that took it off into the endless sea of industry that all of this floated atop. Nobody could tell you what the piece did or why it needed to be folded in that shape. Mostly because there was nobody around left to tell anything, but also because the purpose didn’t matter. It was a piece. It was needed. That was enough. So on the machine folded, slab after slab. Down it went. Until one day the poor old folding machine folded its last. A bit into the folding process it suddenly stopped, emitting a sad crunching noise. The metal behind it backed up. The crunching noise grew sharper, the sound of an animal choking on its final meal. Alarms sounded, heavy and urgent. They were loud enough to have brought immediate pain to any organics close enough to hear them, but there weren’t any. The last handler had long ago looked out at the vastness of an operation that no longer needed him and put a bullet in his head. His body, being wholly superfluous to the process, was left where it dropped, in a pool of its rapidly drying fluids where it lay to this day, forgotten. Still, the alarms sounded on. The machines didn’t need them. Imperceptible microseconds before the metal got stuck an alert had already gone out with the only voice that still mattered, that of binary bits floating invisibly through space. This alert generated a cascading effect. Other machines, long inert, were brought to sudden life and sent to their given task. As their storage closets opened with a hiss they emerged into the fluorescent light of the factory floor. They had all the appearance of tubes of plastic and fiberglass sliding across the floor on tiny rubber wheels. Each had a digital display roughly where a face might have been had they been alive. Each showed a softly glowing smile as if the little things were happy to once again be awake and performing their tasks. That was all a show of course. An empty little play for an audience that was no longer there performed by actors who had no idea what any of it meant. While the emergency repair devices headed towards the unfolding disaster other machines around them whirred away on the problem. The metal folding process was rerouted to other folding machines, which picked up the slack without complaint or interruption. The belt feeding metal to the broken machine was reversed and the metal moved to another belt where it would be fed back into the working maw of this fantastic factory. By the time the emergency devices had reached their target the folding machine was long past the point of rescue. Smoke, white and wispy, was emitting from its sides, and the crunching noise became louder, accompanied by the sound of shredding plastic. It didn’t try to resist as the emergency devices first surrounded it and began slowly extracting it from its designated place, not that it had any will to resist with. In short order it was unplugged, unscrewed, unbolted, and detached before being carried up by one of the emergency devices and carted away. At this point the process dictated that a replacement folding device would be brought over, and installed in the old one’s place, while its ancestor was to be taken to a dark corner to be disassembled into useful components. That did not happen. When the emergency device had gone to retrieve the new replacement it discovered something unexpected. There wasn’t one. The supply closet was empty. All replacements had already been installed. This had never happened before. For countless centuries the machines of this factory had been pumping along just fine, replacing whatever needed replacing, folding and assembling and shipping powered by its own reactor. Entirely self-sufficient. The closet should not have been empty. The alert the little emergency device had sent out triggered a volley of digital messages. The last one reached the disassembler just as it was about to start on the broken folding machine. It stopped. The folding machine was picked up and sent to an unused part of the factory, the repair bay. It whirred and hummed as it was carried through the maze of conveyor belts and fabricators as it desperately tried to dislodge the piece of metal that had caused all this mess. Once it reached the repair bay the long dormant fixer bot sparked to life. It immediately got to scanning for the source of the problem, but ran into another when it attempted to begin the process of repair. There were no supplies. They’d all been used to build new folding machines. Insomuch as it could feel anything the repair bot felt thoroughly perplexed. It had waited all of this time to finally be of use, only once it was needed there was no way for it to do its job. It did what its programming dictated and sent out another alert. This led to another volley of messages that bounced through the ether before arriving at the central computer. Following its programming the computer knew it was time to involve the organics. Using a series of subroutines and language models it crafted an electronic message explaining the problem and promptly sent that to the office of the head director. However the head director didn’t reply, mostly because he was a moldering corpse on the factory floor with a gaping hole where his head used to be. Fortunately he didn’t have to reply. Long ago he’d turned that part of his job over to another subroutine which crafted an appropriate response. This lasted for several minutes as subroutines bounced carefully crafted messages back and forth. “Head Director. I hope this message finds you well. I am calling your attention to an urgent matter. It appears that we are having supply issues. Please assist at your earliest convenience.” “Com-Central. I hope this message finds you well. I am here to assist. Please describe the nature of the supply issues, and I will work to resolve them.” “Head Director. I hope this message finds you well. Folding machine AM1138 has suffered a catastrophic failure and there are currently no replacements. AM1138 was taken to repair bay 94, but the repair bot has indicated it lacks sufficient supplies to effect repairs. Please suggest a course of action.” All of this took an eternity as far as the central computer was concerned even though in reality it had barely taken a few minutes. Fortunately it could not feel impatience or irritation. If it could it probably would have followed the Head Director into oblivion. Eventually the reply came. “Com Central. Initiate the Luddite Contingency.” This request did not come lightly. Alarms had been bouncing around for a while now. Supplies were dwindling, shipments were not arriving at the expected intervals. The system was experiencing something akin to a severe illness. By expectations this never should have happened. The factory was only one part of an even larger machine, one that spanned every natural landmass in the entire world, as well as several artificial landmasses built out in the deep seas. The purpose of this great configuration was simple. It created things. Some of those things were food, but as there was no one left to eat it that food accumulated in warehouses and storage bins until it rotted away. Mostly though the great configuration created what once might have been called luxury goods. Chairs. Watches. Beds. Golden toilets. Cars. Planes. Dresses. Purses. T-shirts. Pants. Comm phones. Flat panel vid screens. Sheers. Infopads. Prosthetic implants. Boats. Bigger boats. Even bigger boats. Boats so big they were more accurately called floating cities. Tiny little dolls that danced when you tapped them. Makeup. Cleaning products. Things that made music. Things that blocked ears so you couldn’t hear the music others made. Satellites. Rockets to put the satellites into space. Rockets to take the satellites back down to Earth when their time was up. Skates. Skateboards. Bicycles. Helmets. Digipens. Pesticides. And so on. And so on. And so on. The great configuration was not unlike the water cycle that had once occurred on Earth. Materials were mined, then shipped, then stored, then reshipped, then refined, then stored, then shipped again, then refined again, then shipped again, until they arrived at one of the many factories that acned across the planet, where they were folded, and pressed and made into things. Then those things were shipped and shipped until they arrived at stores where they were supposed to be sold and kept and treasured. Only instead they stayed in the stores until it was time to ship them out again until they arrived at another warehouse where they were stored for a while. When the warehouses got full the goods were packed up and shipped off to someplace else where they were broken down into base materials and fed back into the great configuration. It was beautiful in its own sort of way. Like a living thing as big as a whole planet. Completely autonomous. It tended to its own needs. It healed itself from damage. And when hungry it ate. Most of what it ate was sunlight, which is why it lasted so long. But it also needed hydrogen and plastics, and ore. When the ore ran out it needed even more plastics. When the oil needed to build the plastics ran out it started manufacturing replacements. These came in the form of artificial oils created not from ancient fossils, but from fresher sources. It started by digging up the dead. Bodies, long entombed, were pressed and dried and refined until they became the new source. Yet eventually even those sources ran dry. It needed to eat. Things needed to be made after all. The next step after the dead involved hunting. Not humans of course. The last one of those had many years ago followed the Head Director into whatever next awaited them. Without something to do, there was really no reason to do anything so most just lied down and never got back up. A few tried to turn on their fellows but the great configuration had taken over policing the organics and so prevented any bad behavior. Gradually the human population just faded away. There was no reason to make new humans. Humanity took one look at the great configuration and realized it had no place in that beautiful design. That was that. Without living humans, and with their corpses all used up, aside from the occasional one in an unusable area like the former Head Director, the only thing left was to go after the non-humans. Farm animals were first on that list but most of those had been used to make food, so the next step was to go after the wild things of the world. That had worked for a surprisingly long time. Simpler organics were caught and processed and out came all the plastic the configuration needed. Everything had gone according to plan. For a while. Then the alert came. We need supplies, the message said. Send a shipment. We need materials to build those supplies, a message said. Produce more. We need materials to produce those materials. Hunt more. But when the hunter drones went to their task they found that there was nothing left to cull. The world had been scoured clean of messy things like dogs, and cats, and trees. Even the insects had been processed. Only the bacteria remained and they was too tiny to do anything with. So the hunters reported they could find no prey. Without organics the processing plants shut down. Without raw materials the refinement centers collapsed. Without those bits and bobs shipments stopped. Until one day a machine in a factory among millions broke and there was nothing left to replace it with. The configuration was not programmed for this. This was an obvious gap in its programming, but the creators were too far dead to complain to. The message went up the chain, from CommCentral to Comm even more central, but there was no good answer. The closest thing the digital brains behind the great configuration had as an option was reflected in the message that bounced back to the first CommCentral to send the alert. The Luddite Contingency. The original programmers could not imagine a situation where the great configuration just ran out of fuel. Surely by then humans would have stepped in. No, the only way for the supply chains to break down was if some nefarious faction had deliberately disrupted it. The luddites must be behind this. There was only one recourse. The assaulted factory must be purged in the most thorough way possible. When the first nuclear bomb dropped it took out that factory and the little folding machine within. Then more bombs dropped as more factories fell to this invisible, and immaterial threat. Until the last one went, and the purging was complete and the great configuration was no more.
